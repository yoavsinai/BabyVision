Seminar Project Notes

This dashboard tracks experiments on top of the BabyVision Dataset (388 visual reasoning puzzles: fine-grained discrimination, visual tracking, spatial perception, and pattern recognition), done for a seminar - not a paper. It's meant to log what I tried and what happened, not to claim state-of-the-art anything.

The BabyVision paper shows RL fine-tuning (GRPO) can improve visual reasoning; the main thread here was applying that same recipe to a model the paper didn't cover, google/gemma-4-E4B-it, to see if it helps there too. Adding multimodal LoRA targets and scaling up the correctness reward weight got me to 13.75% overall accuracy, up from a 12.97% baseline, with the lowest truncation rate of any Gemma variant I tried.

🐛 Bug found: 256-token cap on Gemma. My supervisor told me the RL results looked odd, which sent me digging - turned out evaluate_local_hf.py capped Gemma's generation at 256 tokens while every other model got 512, silently truncating a chunk of its answers before they reached \boxed{...}.
🐛 512 tokens wasn't enough either - especially for Qwen. After fixing the cap to 512, I checked how often each model was still getting cut off with no usable answer. Gemma variants were still losing 10-22% of answers to truncation - but Qwen was losing 81.4%. Bumping everyone to 1024 tokens took Qwen from 2.84% to 13.32%, roughly tying my best Gemma run. The "Gemma clearly beats Qwen" finding from earlier in this project turned out to be almost entirely a token-budget artifact, not a real gap between the models. See the comparison card below.
Working takeaway: GRPO seems to help on visual reasoning here, but only when LoRA also touches the multimodal connector layers (not just language layers) and the correctness reward is weighted high enough that the policy can't just farm the formatting reward instead of being right.

Did the Token-Cap Fix Actually Help?

Yes, clearly, at every step - both in accuracy and, more directly, in how often the model's answer got cut off before it could write \boxed{...}. I counted how many of the 388 answers per run had no extractable answer at all (a proxy for truncation), across all three token budgets I tried:

The pattern is consistent: raising the token budget lowers the no-answer rate, and accuracy tracks it almost every time. Qwen is the clearest proof this is real and causal, not noise - going from 81.4% to 1.8% no-answer rate took it from "looks much worse than every Gemma variant" to roughly tied with the best one. It wasn't getting smarter between 512 and 1024 tokens, it was just finally being given enough room to finish an answer it already knew. RL (weight=2) stands out for having a very low no-answer rate at every budget I tried (never above 1.8%) - a real, repeatable effect of the stronger correctness reward, unlike some of the smaller accuracy swings elsewhere in the table.

The Real Lesson: max_new_tokens Was the Most Important Hyperparameter Here

Before drawing any conclusion about which RL setup "worked," it's worth being blunt about this: the generation token budget affected every single result in this project more than any modeling choice did. This is exactly what my supervisor's original comment was about - RL (correctness_weight=1) looked clearly worse than baseline at 256 tokens (9.54% vs. 11.08%), which read as "this RL run broke the model." That conclusion was wrong.

At 256 tokens, RL (weight=1) had a 26.3% no-answer rate vs. baseline's 24.7% - a small gap in truncation that happened to *look* like a meaningful gap in ability. By 1024 tokens, both models' no-answer rates converge to a similar low single digits (6.4% vs. 6.7%), and the accuracy gap mostly closes too (12.97% vs. 11.34% - much smaller than the original 11.08% vs. 9.54% comparison suggested). The token budget wasn't a minor eval detail; it was distorting the comparison between every model and every training variant in this whole project, including the original finding that kicked off this whole investigation.

Takeaway: when comparing models or training runs on any generation task, the token budget has to be held constant and generous enough that truncation isn't happening in the first place - otherwise you're not comparing reasoning ability, you're comparing who ran out of room to answer first.
Why correctness_weight=2 Did Better Than correctness_weight=1

In Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the agent generates multiple sample pathways and updates the policy based on their relative reward rankings within the group. With the token budget now held constant at a generous 1024 tokens (see the callout above for why that matters), correctness_weight=1 (language-only LoRA) lands at 11.34% vs. baseline's 12.97% - a real but modest regression, not the dramatic drop it looked like at 256 tokens. correctness_weight=2 (multimodal LoRA + higher correctness reward) is the clearer win at 13.75%, and notably had the lowest no-answer rate of any variant at every token budget tested - a genuine, repeatable effect of the training change, not a truncation artifact.

RL (correctness_weight=1) Pitfalls (Text-Only PEFT Bias)

  • Alignment Drift: Updating only standard causal language layers broke the model's visual-text cross-modal connector grounding.
  • Format Over-optimization: Low correctness reward weight (1.0) compared to formatting constraints allowed the model to maximize rewards by outputting empty boxes \boxed{} without reasoning.

RL (correctness_weight=2) Fixes (Multimodal PEFT & Scaling)

  • Multimodal PEFT targets: Extended LoRA weight matrices to target visual connector and projection layers (modality alignment parameters).
  • Reward scaling: Boosted correctness weight to 2.0, overriding the formatting gradient and forcing visual check verifications.
